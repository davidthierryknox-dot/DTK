# Experience > Skills — Drafting Philosophy

## Source (a16z, retained verbatim for fidelity)

> Experience > Skills Charts has already observed that one of the challenges for
> prospective entry-level hires (other than the fact that entry-level hires have always
> been a relatively small share of tech hiring) is that existing tech workers appear to
> be getting older. Why that's the case is some combination of aging-in-place, plus
> remote work unlocking a broader pool of talent, plus perhaps some premium for more
> experienced workers–and while AI may have something to do with it, as well, the
> evidence for that is pretty weak. On the experience point, there's some recent data
> from Revelio Labs that suggests experience is, indeed, becoming a higher priority for
> tech hiring: Since January 2025, tech job postings have seen a marked rise in
> preferred years of experience, with a concomitant drop in preferred number of skills.
> The change is small–~5-10% in both cases–but noticeable, and it's consistent with the
> notion that tech is looking for more experienced workers, rather than workers with any
> particular set of skills, let alone a broad range of skills. That could very well be
> an AI-driven shift: grappling with a new technology means that extant "skills" are
> less applicable, and the new set of skills is still a bit of a whitespace. When you're
> operating on a new frontier, you don't yet know what you'll need, other than the need
> to be able to figure it out–and that's where experience goes a long way. If you look
> at the types of jobs that require the largest premia to experience, it does seem like
> AI is the likely driver. It's data and systems work (and Java) that dominate the
> leaderboard for the recent hiring premium to experience. AI has created new urgency
> around data wrangling, but the shape of wrangled data (and the process of wrangling)
> has changed dramatically, with optimal solutions still very much a moving target. If
> you don't know what needs to be done, then the skillset hasn't been defined yet, and
> someone who can figure it out as they go is the better choice. Eventually, though, as
> implementation matures, one would expect new skills to be defined, and it's likely the
> skills/experience mix will shift again.

## What this means for drafting (mandatory, every CV/CL pass)

1. **Lead with tenure, scope, and outcome — not a skills inventory.** Every bullet opens
   with what was owned/scaled/delivered and over what timeframe, not with a tool name.
   "5+ years managing a 20-30 account Enterprise renewal book, growing net revenue
   through upsell and cross-sell" outranks "Skilled in Salesforce, SAP, forecasting."
2. **Skills keywords are load-bearing but secondary.** They still must hit 100% JD
   coverage (this is non-negotiable per the workflow), but they are woven as evidence
   *inside* experience-driven sentences, never as a bare comma-separated list standing
   in for a track record.
3. **Foreground "figure it out as you go."** When a JD signals a new/ambiguous domain
   (AI-first tooling, e-Invoicing regulatory frontiers, new partner motions), favor
   language that proves adaptive judgment under undefined requirements — Divergent
   Thinking, Structuring Chaos, Negative Capability — over a longer skills list. This is
   exactly the premium the data shows employers now paying for.
4. **Do not pad breadth to compensate for a short list.** A16z's data shows employers
   trimming *number* of skills sought while raising *years* sought. Mirror that: fewer,
   deeper, evidence-backed claims beat a maximalist keyword dump, provided the JD's
   required keywords are still all present (evidence-backed presence, not a skills-list
   appendix).
5. **Years/scope numbers are CRITICAL claims.** Never invent them. Pull them only from
   the user's verified base CV/work history (see Workflow Step 0). If the source
   material doesn't support a number the JD wants, name the closest true equivalent
   experience rather than fabricating a figure.
